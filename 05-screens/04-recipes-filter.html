Recipes & Filter

The screen the research says nobody serves (IA 4). “Suitable for me” is two things at once — the way you eat, and the numbers you’re aiming at — so the filter has two modes on one screen. The mode switches which criteria you tune, never which ones apply, and every card carries its own proof of fit before you tap it. Built only from Design System v1.

  • Stage 5 · Screens, 04 of 05 · revision 2, after one critique
  • Phone frame: 390 × 844 with iPhone safe areas; the mode switch and the chips work
  • Sample recipes and figures are illustrative, never real nutrition data

At a glance

This is the P1 differentiator screen. Revision 1 was generated in one pass from Design System v1 and scored 23/40 in one /impeccable critique run: 0 P0, 2 P1 and 3 P2 issues, all fixed (details below). No new tokens and no new colors were needed — the screen is the first to spend the recipe half of the emphasis dial, gap-section-recipe at 40 px against the 24 of the logging screens.

What the screen does

  • Opens on results, not on a form: both modes arrive pre-filled from what was saved.
  • Filters by the way you eat (tags, allergies) and by per-serving numbers. The mode chooses which set you are tuning; both sets keep applying.
  • Lets you change either for this search only, and hands back a way to undo that.
  • Shows fit on the card: what each recipe adds beyond what you asked for, or the two bounds it comes closest to breaking.

Decided here

  • The list label carries what every result shares; the card carries what differs. The rule the Add Food critique set for search results, applied to filters.
  • A match names your criterion or your margin, never the recipe’s own figure — that figure is already in the strip above it.
  • Every saved criterion holds in both modes (critique P1). The mode switches the controls, not the filter set, and the results label restates the lot.
  • A result card is a row with a 120 px thumbnail, not a poster with a 4:3 image on top (critique P1). The whole card is the link, with no chevron.

Still open

  • The per-meal split behind the pre-filled numbers (a third of the day) has no evidence behind it. It is now stated on screen rather than hidden.
  • The first card’s match lines still fall 46–78 px below the fold on three of the four result frames. Collapsing the filter panel is the v2 move that would close it.
  • Real images: M1 placeholders until the Krea pass.

Why two filter modes, and not one

Dual-mode filtering is the one structural decision this screen exists to make, so it gets its own trace. It is not a designer’s preference: it follows from a definition that was resolved in PRODUCT.md before the IA was written, and that definition came from the audience work.

FindingWhereWhat it forces on this screen
“Suitable for me” means both, not either. “Recipe filtering/search must support both simultaneously (tag-based filters and macro-target matching), since the two segments select recipes on different criteria and neither is a subset of the other.”PRODUCT.md, Capabilities and Constraints; resolved from 02-audience-5w.mdTwo modes on one screen. A single blended filter would serve one segment badly and the other not at all. Both must be first-class, so neither is nested inside the other.

The need is unserved, and provably so. H3: no competitor has a recurring positive pattern around recipe discovery; home cooks visibly leave for external sites; “a whole niche app category exists solely to fill the ‘find a recipe that fits my macros’ gap” (MacroMatch, Macrofy, Stupid Simple Macro Recipes).04-conclusions.md H3; 01-secondary-research.mdRecipes get their own tab in the bottom bar, not a shelf on Today. A separate app category existing for mode 2 alone is independent evidence that a generic recipe list doesn’t serve it.
The incumbents each solve half, or none. MyFitnessPal: 1500+ recipes marketed as tailored to your goals but gated behind the top Premium Plus tier, and its own forum shows users can’t filter even their saved recipes. Lose It!: recipe search with no evidenced goal filtering, which “flags problem recipes retrospectively rather than recommending ones that already fit”. Cal AI: no recipe feature at all.03-competitor-analysis.md, Delighter rowFit must be visible before the tap, which is what the match list on each card is for. And no locked results, no premium badge: paywalling the method users built a habit on was the loudest complaint in the whole review sample (H2).
The handoff is the sharpest differentiator (H4) — a multi-year, still-unresolved request on both MyFitnessPal’s and Cronometer’s forums.04-conclusions.md H4Every card leads to Recipe Detail, which never dead-ends: it resolves into the same Food Confirmation and the same “Log it” as a barcode scan. This screen is the front door to that chain.

Where this stops being evidenced. Research says the two criteria must both exist and that neither contains the other. It does not say they should be two tabs rather than, say, one long stacked form. The mode switch is the same judgment call recorded for Add Food, reused here because the same control already serves IA 2 — and because the two modes are genuinely alternative ways to ask the same question, which is what a tab set is for.

How a card proves fit

The IA asks each result to show “enough to judge fit at a glance (name, image, calories/serving, matched criteria)”. Getting that right took one rule, because the obvious version repeats itself.

ProblemRuleOn the screen
Filters are AND, so under a naïve reading every card would list the same matched criteria, four times over.The list label names what every result shares; a card names only what differs. This is not new: the Add Food critique set exactly this rule for search results, where every row shared one source.“4 recipes · vegetarian · high-protein · no shellfish” once, above the list.
A match that repeats the recipe’s own figure duplicates the nutrition strip two lines above it, and the same number then appears twice meaning two different things.A match names your criterion or your margin, never the recipe’s figure.Tag mode: the tags this one adds beyond your filter (“Vegan”, “Gluten-free”). Macro mode: the room it leaves (“170 kcal under your limit”).
In macro mode the extras are figures, and figures carry a brand rule.L1: every figure is Inter, tabular, Ink, never serif, never tinted or faded — including inside a match line.“170 kcal under your limit”: the number is figure-sm, the words are ui-secondary.
Critique P2. Macro mode filters on four bounds but the card reported only two of them, by no stated rule — so a planner who had just tuned the fat field got no feedback on fat.A card names the two bounds it clears by the least, tightest first: where it comes closest to breaking one of your rules.The cottage-cheese bake leads with fat (18 of 20 g); the tofu bowl leads with protein (1 g clear). In frame D, raising protein to 48 g makes protein the tighter bound on the surviving recipe, and it moves to the front.
Critique P2. Nothing said how results were ordered, and the sample data obeyed no order.Best fit first, said in the label. In macro mode “fit” is the room a recipe has against its tightest bound; in tag mode it is how many of your other criteria it also meets.“3 recipes, best fit first …”. The build sorts the data by that rule and asserts the order, so the notes and the pixels cannot disagree.
Critique P1, and the sharpest finding of the run. Revision 1 let the mode switch drop every diet criterion except allergies: frames C and D showed chicken and salmon to a user whose saved preference is vegetarian, while the header still claimed “Filtered to how you eat”.The mode switches which criteria you tune, never which ones apply. Every saved criterion holds in both modes, and the results label restates all of them in both.“3 recipes, best fit first · vegetarian · high-protein · no shellfish · inside your per-serving targets”. The macro-mode results were replaced with vegetarian dishes that meet the same numbers.

The tension this leaves. The diet criteria now apply in macro mode but their controls are not visible there — the chips live in the tag panel, as IA 4 specifies, so in macro mode the criteria are stated in the results label and changed one tab away. That is the smallest honest arrangement that keeps IA 4’s grouping and still keeps the promise; lifting the chips above the switch would be an IA change and is flagged rather than made silently. The other alternative, worth testing: results that match most of your criteria, ranked, each card naming the one it misses. That needs a “not matched” value in the match list, and it risks showing a home cook a dish that breaks the one rule they care about.

Left off the screen, on purpose

Left offWhy
Generic recipe browsing: collections, “what’s popular”, meal-type tabs“Generic un-filtered recipe browsing” is named in 04-conclusions.md §2 as a Nice-to-have explicitly excluded from this project’s scope. It is also the thing the research says users already leave apps to do elsewhere.
Searching recipes by nameIA 4 is a filter, not a search. Add Food owns search, for foods. A name search would serve someone who already knows what they want to cook, which is not either job story.
Sort controls, ratings, cook time, difficulty, saving or favouritingNone is in the IA 4 content list, and none helps judge fit. Results are ordered by fit, which is the only ranking the screen claims.
A premium badge, locked results, an upgrade promptGating goal-filtered recipes behind a top tier is precisely what MyFitnessPal does, and paywalling a built habit drew the loudest complaint in the review sample (H2). Monetization is undecided; this screen does not pre-decide it.
A separate filter screen, with results behind an “Apply” buttonThe IA merged them: splitting adds a step without adding evidenced value, and results updating in place is what makes tuning a number feel like tuning rather than submitting.
Per-recipe daily-percentage barsMyFitnessPal’s recipe view shows “Percent of Daily Goals” per macro. It is the right idea in the wrong place: it lives inside an opened recipe. The same information belongs on the card, as a margin against the target you set, which is what the match list does.

What the reference screens show

All twelve Mobbin screens in 03-branding/refs/ were looked at again for this screen. Not one of them is a recipe browsing or filtering screen — which is the visual form of H3, and means the layout here is composed from system parts rather than borrowed.

  • mfp_nutr · MyFitnessPal, Recipe Details. The closest thing in the set: “Percent of Daily Goals” with a bar and a percentage for calories, carbs, fat and protein, then a nutrient ledger, then “LOG TO DIARY” pinned full width. Taken: the idea that a recipe should be readable against your goals, and right-aligned figures. Left: its position — this is fit shown after you commit to opening a recipe, which is the research’s “flags problem recipes retrospectively rather than recommending ones that already fit”, seen directly. Moving that proof onto the card is this screen’s whole argument.
  • lifesum_and · Lifesum, Dietary needs & preferences. Food preferences (vegan, vegetarian, pescatarian) as checkboxes, then allergies (nuts, fish, shellfish, egg, milk, lactose, gluten, wheat) as switches, on a settings screen with a Save button. Taken: the two-group split, and the vocabulary. Left: the whole pattern of a separate settings screen disconnected from browsing — here the same data is chips on the filter itself, pre-filled and editable per search, so there is nothing to save.
  • lifesum_goal · Lifesum, goal question. Oat ground, Paper cards edge to edge, a serif headline, generous vertical rhythm. Taken, via the system: this is Direction B, the cookbook half of the hybrid, and it is what gap-section-recipe at 40 is a formalisation of. Left: the shadowed cards — v1 separates by spacing and hairlines, not elevation.
  • mfp_dash, mfp_log, mfp_scr · MyFitnessPal. Label-left, figure-right rows; a blue “PREMIUM 👑” badge in the header; “8 days!” in blue. Taken: ledger alignment, already C2 and N1. Left: the premium badge, which is the paywall signal H2 names, and the reward colour.
  • calai1, yazio1, macro1, macro2, noom1, lifesum2. Streak flames, mascots, tutorial cards, an orange expenditure chart, brand splash screens. Nothing for this screen. Direction C’s gamification and illustration were excluded in Stage 3, and nothing here argues for reopening that.

Heuristics, applied while building

Nielsen’s ten heuristics served as constraints during generation; the single /impeccable critique run tested them afterwards.

HeuristicApplied as
1 · Visibility of system statusThe count and the criteria behind it sit above the list and announce themselves when they change. The selected mode is a filled segment, a chosen chip carries a check, and “Reset to my usual” appearing beside the count is itself a status: you have changed something.
2 · Match with the real world“How you eat”, “Avoid”, “Per serving”, “Calories, at most”, “105 kcal under your max”. No symbols standing in for words, and no “filters applied: 3”.
3 · User control and freedomEvery filter is editable for this search only, and “Reset to my usual” undoes the lot from one fixed place beside the count. Nothing here writes to the profile, so exploring costs nothing.
4 · Consistency and standardsThe same mode switch as Add Food, the same chips, the same figure treatment as the logging screens, the same bottom bar as Today. The list label follows the rule set for search results.
5 · Error preventionField labels say which way a bound runs, so “Protein, at least 40” can’t be read as a ceiling. Every saved criterion holds in both modes — revision 1 dropped all but the allergy and showed a vegetarian chicken, the one failure that would cost this screen its whole argument.
6 · Recognition over recallThe criteria sit above the results and are restated in the label, in both modes, so a card is never read against something remembered. Margins are stated rather than left to subtraction. Limit: the filter and the label are static, so once you scroll to compare they leave the screen — a sticky label row needs the elevation token v1 deferred, so it is recorded for v2.
7 · Flexibility and efficiencyThe screen opens on results. A returning user who wants their usual answer does nothing at all; a planner who wants to tune one number touches one field.
8 · Aesthetic and minimalist designFour things per card, one of them a thumbnail rather than a poster — the critique found the image was 66% of the card and cost the list its scannability. No ratings, times, badges or scores. Shared criteria stated once, not on every card.
9 · Recognize and recover from errorsNo match is a message that names the figure doing it and the ones worth loosening, with a button back to the saved targets.
10 · Help and documentationOne line, where a number is derived rather than saved: “About a third of your day …” under the per-serving fields. The critique was right that this was the figure a planner most wants to audit and the one thing the screen would not explain. Everywhere else the labels do the work.

Detector findings

16 findings, none real, and 14 of them are the same rules the 03 Food Confirmation run already triaged. False positives (13): cramped-padding ×12, every one of them in presentation chrome below the “not part of the screen or the system” marker — the bay, the five device frames and the six notes sections; no element inside a .screen was flagged. flat-type-hierarchy ×1, because the scanner cannot resolve the var(--type-*) shorthands and falls back to an inherited 16 px; measured in the browser inside .screen, the scale runs 26/32 → 20/26 → 16/24 → 14/20 → 12/16, a largest adjacent step of 1.30:1 against a 1.25:1 target. Deliberate (3): the Paper brand colour (cream-palette), the M1 placeholder’s stripes (repeating-stripes-gradient), and five aphoristic-cadence matches that are all in these notes, not in product copy. What this screen proves is about placement, not proportion: a 4:3 image on top of a card cost 262 of 398 px and made the list unscannable.
